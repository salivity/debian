The Debian Social Contract: The Foundation of Debian

The Debian Social Contract defines the moral agenda and philosophical commitments of the Debian Project, serving as a pledge between the community of developers and the broader world of software users. This article explores the core components of the Debian Social Contract—including its five primary commitments and the Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG)—and examines why this document is regarded as the enduring foundation upon which the Debian operating system is developed, maintained, and governed.

What Constitutes the Debian Social Contract?

Adopted in 1997, the Debian Social Contract is divided into two primary sections: the core commitments to the Free Software community, and the Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG).

The first section outlines Debian’s public commitments:

  1. Debian will remain 100% free: The core operating system and all its integrated components will always adhere strictly to Free Software principles.
  2. We will give back to the free software community: Bug fixes, improvements, and upstream code are shared back with original authors and the open-source community at large.
  3. We will not hide problems: Debian maintains open, publicly accessible bug databases and transparent developmental processes.
  4. Our priorities are our users and free software: Development is guided primarily by the needs of the user base and the advancement of software freedom, rather than commercial interests.
  5. Works that do not meet our free software standards: While the core system is completely free, Debian acknowledges that some users require non-free software, providing specialized repositories (contrib and non-free) that are not enabled by default.

The second section, the Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG), establishes the objective criteria used to determine whether a software license is truly "free." It mandates unrestricted redistribution, access to source code, permission for modifications and derived works, and an explicit prohibition of discrimination against any person, group, or field of endeavor.

Why It Is the Foundation of Debian

The Debian Social Contract is not merely a statement of intent; it functions as the operational constitution of the Debian project.

First, it establishes strict standards for system packaging. The DFSG dictates what can enter the official Debian distribution (main). If a piece of software violates even a single guideline—such as restricting commercial use or concealing source code—it is barred from the primary distribution. This guarantees that any user installing Debian receives a fully auditable, redistributable, and non-proprietary system.

Second, it maintains project autonomy and governance. Debian is not owned or governed by a single corporate entity. The Social Contract unifies a decentralized, global network of volunteer developers under a single, non-negotiable set of shared values. Any structural modifications to the contract require a supermajority vote among Debian developers via a General Resolution.

Finally, the document serves as the historical catalyst for modern open-source licensing. The DFSG directly formed the basis of the Open Source Definition created by the Open Source Initiative (OSI). By establishing clear, ethical, and legal boundaries for software freedom, the Debian Social Contract ensures the stability, transparency, and integrity that have made Debian the base for dozens of other operating systems, including Ubuntu, Linux Mint, and Kali Linux.
